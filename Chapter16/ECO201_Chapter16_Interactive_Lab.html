Meeting 1 · Slides 5–7

Trust signals and the Used Car Marketplace

Learn how buyers and sellers reduce information gaps. Then choose a used car before the hidden repair costs are known.

About 15–18 minutes

Experiment 1 · Choose a way to reduce uncertainty

Information is not equally available to everyone. Some tools tell buyers more about quality; others protect a seller or lender when payment is uncertain.

Reputation & reviews

Evidence from earlier customers or repeat business.

Money-back guarantee

Refund when specific conditions are met.

Warranty

Repair or replacement for a stated period.

Service contract

Extra paid coverage for specified repairs.

License or certification

Evidence of training or demonstrated qualifications.

Collateral or cosigner

Protection for a lender if a borrower fails to repay.

Important distinction: A warranty and inspection can reduce buyer concerns about a product; collateral and a cosigner protect a lender against nonpayment. They do not establish that the item itself is high quality.

The assumed prices and participation are illustrative—not an empirical estimate or a full equilibrium model. When inspections are available, this example assumes buyers pay $250 per car to verify quality.

How does this illustrate an information problem?

When sellers know more than buyers, buyers may not pay enough for genuinely higher-quality goods. Some good sellers may exit, leaving a thinner market with fewer choices. A trustworthy inspection makes it possible to distinguish quality and trade both types. In practice, inspections can be imperfect and are valuable only when their benefits exceed their costs.

View the assumptions behind the custom model

Initial population: 80 lower-risk drivers with a 5% annual chance of one claim and 40 higher-risk drivers with a 15% chance. A claim produces a $3,000 covered loss.

Illustrative enrollment: At premiums up to $250, all 80 lower-risk drivers stay. At $275–$350, 40 stay; at $375–$450, 20 stay; above $450, none stay. All 40 higher-risk drivers stay through $500; above $500, 20 stay. These thresholds are invented solely for classroom exploration.

Cost sharing: On each $3,000 covered claim, the insured first pays the deductible, then the chosen coinsurance on the remaining balance. For simplicity, the simulation does not change enrollment or claim probabilities when cost sharing changes. The moral-hazard checkbox changes only the higher-risk group’s probability.

Profit caution: Expected result = premium revenue minus expected insurer claim payments. It is not actual profit: operating expenses, investment income, taxes, and reserves are excluded. Values are expected averages, not guarantees.

Instructor notes and example answers

Module 1: Demand shifts left when uncertain quality reduces buyers’ willingness to pay at a given price. When sellers leave too, the price effect depends on the relative shifts. Module 2: Match signals to uncertainty problems; then choose any car before revealing repairs. After repairs, A costs $9,000; B costs $8,000; C costs $9,200. B is least expensive on this narrow immediate-cost measure. Ask how future risk, inspection, and warranty terms could change a buyer’s choice.

Module 3: Round 1: (80×0.05 + 40×0.15)×$3,000 ÷120 = $250. Round 2: (40×0.05 + 40×0.15)×$3,000 ÷80 = $300 (adverse selection). Round 3: (40×0.05 + 40×0.20)×$3,000 ÷80 = $375 (moral hazard assumption). Quick check: $200 + 20% of $800 = $360 paid by customer; $640 by insurer.

Accessibility: Controls are keyboard-operable, graphical changes have numerical text equivalents, and the three modules have separate headings and landmarks. The simulation does not depend on color alone. The downloadable text summary is usable as a student response record.
