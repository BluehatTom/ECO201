Why price wars can persist

  1. Mutual dependence: the best choice depends on what the rival does.
  2. Dominant strategy: here, cutting yields a higher payoff for Firm A whether B maintains high prices or cuts. The same holds for B.
  3. Nash equilibrium: at (cut, cut), neither firm can improve profit by switching alone.
  4. Prisoner's dilemma: both could earn more at (high, high), but each has an incentive to undercut the other.
Joint profit (high, high) = $160
Joint profit (cut, cut) = $90
Individual incentive to cut when rival is high: $110 > $80

This is an abstract game. In real markets, pricing outcomes depend on costs, product differences, entry barriers, repeated interactions, and law. Explicit price-fixing agreements are illegal under U.S. antitrust law.
